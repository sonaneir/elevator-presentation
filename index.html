The problem

Stairs or elevator?

Option A · Stairs

k min

Always the same time. Known in advance.

Option B · Elevator

00:00arrives at m = ?

The ride takes 1 minute, but the arrival time m is unknown.

Every minute you decide: keep waiting or give up and walk, without knowing the future.

Imagine you are on the ground floor. You can take the stairs — that always takes k minutes. Or you can wait for the elevator. The ride itself takes one minute, but you don't know when the elevator will arrive; let's call that time m. Every minute you have to decide: keep waiting, or give up and walk. If you knew m, the choice would be easy. The whole problem is that you don't.

Key idea

Online vs. offline

Offline — knows m

OPT(m) = min(m+1, k)

The ideal benchmark. Impossible in real life.

Online — doesn’t know m

ratio = max ALG / OPT

Competitive ratio: how much worse than ideal in the worst case.

The simplest rent-or-buy (ski rental) problem: waiting is renting minute by minute, the stairs are buying once.

An offline algorithm knows the whole input in advance. Here it knows m, so its cost is simply the minimum of m plus one and k. We call it OPT. Our algorithm is online — it doesn't know m. To measure how good it is, we use the competitive ratio: the worst-case ratio between our cost and OPT. A ratio of 2 means we are never more than twice as slow as someone who knows the future. This problem is the simplest case of rent-or-buy, also known as ski rental: waiting is like renting by the minute, the stairs are like buying once.

Any deterministic strategy is just a threshold — how long you are willing to wait. Two extremes. Always take the stairs: if the elevator was already standing there, we spent k minutes instead of one — k times worse. Always wait: the elevator might take an hour, so the ratio is unbounded. The classical algorithm is in between — the break-even strategy — and its ratio is less than 2.

The break-even rule

Wait k − 1 minutes.
Then take the stairs.

If the elevator arrives at minute k − 1, riding costs exactly k — the same as the stairs. After that, waiting can never pay off.

Here is the rule: wait k minus one minutes. If the elevator hasn't come — take the stairs. Why k minus one? If the elevator arrives at minute k minus one, riding it costs exactly k — the same as the stairs. After that point, waiting can never beat walking. That is the break-even point.

Analysis

Never worse than 2× the ideal

Case 1: m ≤ k − 1

Ride the elevator: cost m + 1 = OPT. Ratio 1.

Case 2: m ≥ k

Wait k − 1, walk k: cost 2k − 1, while OPT = k.

ratio = 2 − 1/k

No deterministic threshold does better: the adversary sends the elevator right after you leave. Memory O(1), at most k checks.

Break-evenOptimum OPTAlways stairsAlways wait
Two cases. If the elevator comes in time, we ride it and we are exactly optimal. If it doesn't, we waited k minus one and walked k — that's 2k minus 1, while the ideal is k. So the ratio is 2 minus 1 over k — always below 2. On the chart, the blue line follows the optimum until minute k minus 1, then stays flat. Let me move the slider to show it. And no deterministic strategy can do better: an adversary can always send the elevator one minute after we leave. The algorithm uses constant memory and at most k checks.

ELEVATOR-ARRIVED(t) is the algorithm’s only view of the future.

Here is the pseudocode in the style of our textbook. t counts the minutes. While t is at most k minus one, we check whether the elevator has arrived. If yes, we ride and return t plus one. If not, we wait one more minute. If the loop ends, we take the stairs and return k minus one plus k. Let's trace it. With k equal to 5 and the elevator at minute 3, the algorithm returns 4. If the elevator never comes, it returns 9 — that is 2k minus 1.

Real-world application

A thread waiting for a lock

The thread does not know when the lock will be released: the same online problem.

Spin = wait for the elevator
CPU burns while waiting
zz
Block = take the stairs
fixed context-switch cost B
⇢
Break-even spin ≈ B, then block

Never wastes more than about 2× the ideal CPU time. Used by OS kernels and the JVM.

Where is this used? In multithreaded programs. A thread wants a lock that another thread holds. It can spin — keep checking, burning CPU, but continue instantly. Or it can block — ask the operating system to put it to sleep, which costs a fixed amount of time for the context switch. The thread doesn't know when the lock will be released — exactly our problem. The break-even rule says: spin about as long as blocking costs, then block. Operating systems and virtual machines use this spin-then-block idea.
